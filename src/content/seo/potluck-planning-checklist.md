A potluck should make sharing the meal easier, not leave everyone guessing what to bring. Set the shape of the meal, then invite guests to fill in useful pieces. A simple assignment list helps avoid five desserts and no serving spoon.

## 1. Decide what the host is providing

Choose the parts that give the meal a foundation. The host might provide a main dish and drinks, while guests bring sides or dessert. Provide plates and utensils if you can.

Decide the basics first:

- Date, arrival time, and when you expect to eat.
- Guest count and whether children or additional guests are included.
- What the host will provide.
- Whether the food will be set out as a buffet, served at a table, or kept casual.
- How guests should share dietary needs or questions.

Keep the plan within the host’s space and capacity. A few varied contributions are easier to coordinate than a long list of requirements.

## 2. Assign categories before exact dishes

If everyone picks independently, contributions can bunch together. Offer broad categories, then confirm each dish. Ask one guest for a vegetable side, another for bread, and another for dessert. Keep a few open slots for guests who prefer to choose.

Use a list that is easy to scan:

| Category | Person | Dish | Serving notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main | Host | Baked pasta | Host provides serving spoon |
| Side | Maya | Roasted vegetables | Bring in a covered dish |
| Bread | Luis | Rolls | Host has a basket |
| Dessert | Open | To be confirmed | Needs a serving utensil |

Track the owner, dish, and serving needs. If two guests offer similar dishes, ask whether one wants to switch or keep the duplicate. Check before changing an assignment.

## 3. Ask the useful follow-up questions

Once a dish is chosen, confirm:

- Roughly how many people it is meant to serve.
- Whether it needs reheating, refrigeration, or counter space.
- Whether the guest is bringing its serving spoon, tongs, or other gear.
- When it will arrive and whether the guest needs help unloading it.
- Whether the dish name or ingredients should be shared with the group.

Ask guests directly about dietary needs and preferences, and explain what the host can accommodate. Confirm ingredients and preparation with the person bringing each dish, then share the information.

## 4. Plan the serving setup

Clear a table or counter, decide where dishes will go, and make room for people to move through. Check plates, cups, napkins, and utensils. If you are short, tell guests what reusable items to bring.

Tell guests where to put dishes. Keep labels and a marker nearby.

## 5. Send one message with the full plan

Copy and adapt this note so guests see the same details:

> We’re having a potluck at our place on [day, date]. Come by at [arrival time]; we’ll eat around [meal time]. We’re providing [host contributions]. Could you bring [category] to share with about [number] people, plus a serving utensil if the dish needs one? Let me know what you’re thinking, whether it needs fridge or oven space, and if anything changes. The address and arrival details are: [add details]. Please message me directly with any dietary needs or questions.

Edit the date, timing, menu, and portions. Include a response deadline if you need to finalize shopping. Tell affected people when a dish or timing changes, then revise the list so everyone can find the current plan.

## 6. Confirm the day before

Check the list for gaps or duplicates. Confirm dishes, serving gear, arrival time, and address. If someone cancels, offer the open category to the group and keep the meal simple enough to adjust.

## Keep shared planning clear

If a few friends are organizing the meal, use [Gatherory for iPhone](/iphone) to keep their dish ideas and preparation tasks with the event. Confirm contributions directly with the people bringing food, and send guests the current arrival and serving instructions.

With the plan settled, leave some room for the unexpected. A potluck is a shared meal, and the evening matters more than matching every dish to a perfect menu.

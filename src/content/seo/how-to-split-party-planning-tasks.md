## Agree on the plan before dividing the work

Start with a short conversation about the party: its purpose, likely guest count, date range, budget boundaries, and any practical limits. These are planning choices. If the group has not settled them, do not quietly turn one person’s preference into a task for everyone else.

Separate decisions from work. “Choose a date that works for the hosts” is a decision; “send the selected date to guests by Friday” is a task. A task can move forward once the people with authority have made the choice it depends on.

Name who can make which decisions. For example, a host may have the final say on the home and guest capacity, while cohosts agree together on the theme or activity. If a decision affects the budget or another person’s availability, bring it to the group before committing.

## Give each task one owner

Every task should have one person responsible for moving it forward, even when others help. “We’ll handle snacks” sounds shared but does not tell anyone who will check the list or notice if it is unfinished. One owner can ask for help, coordinate details, and report status.

Define “done” in observable terms. “Plan music” could mean choosing a playlist and confirming who will bring a speaker. “Get decorations” could mean selecting items within an agreed limit and having them at the venue before setup begins. A useful definition prevents two people from assuming the other has finished.

Set due dates around dependencies. Invitations need a confirmed date and location first. Setup supplies need to arrive before the setup window. A due date should help the next person act, not just fill a calendar.

## Example: three cohosts planning a birthday

Here is one way to divide a small party. Adjust the owners to match people’s availability and strengths.

| Owner | Responsibility | Definition of done | Handoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alex | Guest communication | Sends the confirmed time, address, and reply-by date | Shares the final guest count with Sam by the planning check-in |
| Sam | Food coordination | Collects contributions and notes serving needs | Gives Jordan the setup list before the event |
| Jordan | Space and setup | Confirms seating, entry notes, and setup supplies | Walks the host through the room plan before guests arrive |

All three can weigh in on the party’s overall feel and raise concerns. That does not mean each person must own every task. If one responsibility becomes larger than expected, the owner should say so while there is still time to rebalance it.

## Balance the load fairly

Count effort, not just assignments. One task may take ten minutes; another may involve several calls, purchases, and follow-ups. Consider each person’s schedule, access to the venue or car, comfort with the task, and other commitments. Ask before assigning work that depends on someone’s money, home, equipment, or availability.

Make invisible work visible. Tracking replies, reminding people about a deadline, comparing options, and updating the plan all take attention. Rotate that coordination work or acknowledge it when you review the workload.

Leave room for someone to decline or trade a task. A fair plan is one the group can actually complete, not one where every person has the same number of rows.

## Make handoffs explicit

When a task moves from one person to another, include the information needed to continue: current status, decisions already made, items still unresolved, links or receipts if relevant, and the next deadline. Say who is taking the next step and when they need it.

If one task depends on another, write down the connection. For example, the person setting up the room may need the final guest count from the invitation owner. A handoff date gives both people a chance to spot a delay before it affects the party.

Keep one shared list with task, owner, due date, status, and blocker. A group chat can carry quick conversation, but the current plan should not depend on scrolling back through messages.

## Use a short check-in

Set a brief check-in at a point when decisions or dependencies are coming due. Ask each owner: What is complete? What is next? What is blocked? Does anything need a decision or a different owner?

You can send this prompt:

> Quick planning check-in: Please share your task status, the next step, and any blocker by [time]. If you need a decision from the group, include the options and when you need an answer. We’ll adjust owners or timing together if the workload has changed.

Keep the check-in focused on removing blockers. It is not a performance review, and a delayed task is a reason to adjust the plan, not to shame the person.

## Keep the plan and decisions visible

[Gatherory for iPhone](/iphone) keeps ideas, individual preferences, tasks, costs, and the schedule with an event. **Pass**, **Maybe**, and **Love It** record each planner’s input; they do not approve an idea automatically. A planner with permission makes that decision separately. The guide to [team decisions](/how-team-decisions-work) explains that distinction.

For a small group, the useful habit is the same in any tool: put the approved plan where collaborators can find it, give work a clear owner, and revisit assignments when circumstances change. Before the event, confirm that every important task has an owner and that the final handoffs have reached the people who need them.

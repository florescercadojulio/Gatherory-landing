## Choose a checklist for your gathering

Use this general sequence for the whole plan, or start with an example closer to your event:

- [Birthday dinner checklist](/birthday-dinner-planning-checklist): meal, birthday details, helping jobs, and an evening timeline.
- [Surprise birthday checklist](/surprise-birthday-party-checklist): guest arrival instructions, secrecy, and a fallback.
- [Dinner party checklist](/dinner-party-planning-checklist): a manageable menu, prep, serving, and cleanup.
- [Potluck checklist](/potluck-planning-checklist): contributions, portions, serving gear, and changes.
- [Friendsgiving checklist](/friendsgiving-planning-checklist): holiday schedules, a shared meal, and a welcoming space.

Planning with others? [Split the tasks with clear owners and handoffs](/how-to-split-party-planning-tasks) before the work piles up.

## 1. Define the event before filling the calendar

This is a planning checklist, not a list of automated app features. Contracts, payments, reminders, and vendor arrangements may need separate tools.

- Write one sentence explaining why the event exists.
- Name the primary host or decision-makers.
- Estimate the number of guests.
- Set an initial budget cap and decide who can approve spending.
- List any immovable dates, accessibility requirements, or location constraints.
- Decide whether the event is private, invitation-only, or public.

The purpose should guide later decisions. A relaxed family dinner and a public fundraiser may have the same number of attendees, but they need completely different planning systems.

## 2. Create one shared source of truth

- Choose where dates, locations, ideas, decisions, people, and notes will live.
- Give collaborators access to that place instead of forwarding screenshots.
- Keep sensitive planning details separate from the guest-facing experience.
- Agree on how a suggestion becomes an approved decision.
- Record who owns each next step.

A group chat is useful for conversation, but it is a poor archive. Important context moves upward, links lose their meaning, and the same question gets answered more than once.

## 3. Shape the budget around real priorities

- Identify the two or three parts of the event that matter most.
- Reserve a contingency amount before allocating everything else.
- Estimate venue, food, travel, entertainment, photography, decor, and accessibility costs as relevant.
- Record deposits, due dates, cancellation terms, and remaining balances.
- Revisit the budget when a major idea is approved.

Budget conversations are clearer when the expense is connected to the event or decision it supports.

## 4. Choose the date and place

- Collect a short list of realistic dates.
- Confirm availability with essential participants.
- Compare venues on capacity, accessibility, location, restrictions, weather backup, and total cost.
- Document arrival instructions, parking, transit, and accommodations.
- Confirm the time zone for guests or collaborators planning from elsewhere.
- Save contracts and contact details in a predictable location.

Do not treat a tentative venue as confirmed in guest communication. Mark what is still undecided so collaborators do not build on a false assumption.

## 5. Map every connected event

Many gatherings include more than one moment. Add the rehearsal, setup window, tasting, welcome dinner, ceremony, reception, after-party, next-day brunch, or travel block as separate connected events when they have their own logistics.

For each event, record:

- purpose and status;
- date, time, and time zone;
- location;
- responsible host or collaborator;
- participant list;
- budget allocation;
- guest visibility; and
- unresolved decisions.

## 6. Build the people plan

- Create the initial guest list.
- Distinguish hosts, planning collaborators, vendors, and guests.
- Decide whether plus-ones or children are included.
- Collect accessibility, dietary, travel, and contact information only when needed.
- Assign collaborators a specific responsibility and deadline.
- Review who can see private notes, budgets, addresses, and unfinished ideas.

In Gatherory, share guest access for attendance and collaborator access for planning. Review collaborator-link permissions and whether approval is required before you send the link. Pending requests appear in **Event Settings → People & Access → Join Requests**.

Inviting someone into the planning process is different from inviting them to attend. Use roles that preserve that difference.

## 7. Turn ideas into decisions

- Capture options before evaluating them.
- Add the information needed to compare each option: cost, location, availability, fit, and owner.
- Set a decision date for anything blocking other work.
- Record the chosen option and why it won.
- Move approved event ideas into the schedule or action plan.
- Archive rejected ideas so they stop competing for attention.

This is where many plans stall. A saved idea feels like progress, but it only becomes useful when the group knows whether it is still being considered.

Gatherory’s **Pass**, **Maybe**, and **Love It** choices collect personal input. They do not approve or delete an idea. Review the team’s responses, including unanswered input, before an authorized planner approves it separately.

## 8. Build the schedule backward

- Start with the event’s fixed start time.
- Add setup, vendor arrival, host arrival, guest arrival, transitions, and cleanup.
- Include travel time between locations.
- Assign an owner to every critical handoff.
- Add a private host timeline and a simpler guest schedule.
- Confirm backup plans for weather, delays, or missing materials.

Leave margin. A schedule with no breathing room becomes inaccurate after the first late arrival.

## 9. Prepare the guest experience

- Confirm the event name, date, time, location, dress guidance, and RSVP deadline.
- Send invitations only after the core details are stable.
- Explain what guests need to bring, know, or do.
- Share only the events and details relevant to each guest.
- Plan reminders for the RSVP deadline and final logistics.
- Make important accessibility and contact information easy to find.

Read the invitation as someone who has seen none of the planning conversation. If it assumes hidden context, revise it.

Check **Who’s Coming** for each event’s RSVP status: **Going**, **Not Sure**, or **Not Going**. Pull down to refresh before relying on the latest attendance count.

## 10. Run the final review

One week before the event:

- confirm vendors, reservations, quantities, and final balances;
- review attendance and outstanding responses;
- share the final schedule with hosts and collaborators;
- check weather and backup plans;
- prepare contact lists, materials, signage, and emergency items; and
- decide who can make event-day decisions if the primary host is unavailable.

On the day, keep one current plan. Last-minute changes should reach the people affected without forcing everyone else to reread the entire event.

## After the event

- Confirm final payments and returns.
- Gather photos or shared memories.
- Send thanks to guests and collaborators.
- Record notes that would help with a future event.
- Archive sensitive information that no longer needs to remain active.

An event may end in a day, but a clear record of what happened makes the next gathering easier.
